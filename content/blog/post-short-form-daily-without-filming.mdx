Every solo builder has had the same Sunday-night thought: *I should be posting.* The app is good, the
landing page is fine, and the only channel that reliably sends strangers to a product in 2026 is a
short vertical video that plays before someone decides to keep scrolling.

Then Monday arrives, the bug tracker is open, and the camera stays in the drawer. Again.

This is not a discipline problem. It is a time-arithmetic problem, and it has a structural fix.

## The arithmetic nobody says out loud

A "quick" TikTok, made the way creators make them, costs roughly:

- 20 minutes deciding what it is about
- 15 minutes filming three takes
- 30 minutes cutting, captioning, picking a sound
- 10 minutes writing the caption and hashtags, then posting

Call it 75 minutes. Four a week is five hours. That is a working day, every week, taken from the thing
that actually makes your product better. And the return on any single post is close to zero, because
short-form is a volume game: the platform's recommendation model needs dozens of posts before it knows
who to show you to.

So most builders do one of two things. They post twice, see twelve views, and stop. Or they never start.
Both are rational responses to the arithmetic.

## Change what you do, not how much of it

The fix is to move yourself to the one step in the process that genuinely needs a human, and let code
do the rest. In a video, the parts that need you are small:

1. **Judgement.** Is this hook true to the product? Would I say this?
2. **Approval.** Yes or no.

Everything else is mechanical once the judgement is made: laying out a slide, timing a caption to a
sentence, mixing a voice track, uploading at 17:30 on Tuesday. Mechanical work is what software is for.

Here is the workflow in full, the way Friday runs it:

**Paste your product's URL once.** The site is read like a careful new hire would read it: what it does,
who it is for, what tone the copy already uses. That becomes a brief you can edit. It is written once and
cached, so there is no per-video "prompting".

**Every batch starts from something that already worked.** Instead of asking a model to invent a video,
each draft is built on a template curated by a human from a short that genuinely broke out in your niche.
The template carries the shape: the hook pattern, the beat structure, the pacing. Only the words are new,
and they are written about *your* product.

**Ten drafts land in a feed. You swipe.** Right to keep, left to skip. A skip costs you nothing. A keep
triggers the full render: voice, captions timed to the sentence, product screenshots as b-roll, your
photo opening the video if you added one.

**Kept videos post themselves.** Pick a slot in the calendar or take the suggested one. TikTok and Shorts
get the right format each, at the right time, with a tracked link in the caption so you learn which post
sent people to your product.

That is the whole thing. The week in the illustration above is not a fantasy schedule. It is Tuesday,
Thursday, Friday, Sunday, one swipe each, and the founder's calendar full of building.

## Three formats, so it never looks like a template

Volume only works if the feed does not look like the same post ten times. Friday drafts in three shapes
and mixes them in every batch:

</Figure>

- **Slideshows** are the cheapest to make and, on TikTok, often the best performing for a founder story:
  six frames, one line each, narrated in your chosen voice.
- **Hook videos** put the product on screen. A bold line for two seconds, then real screenshots with
  captions timed to the voice. These cross-post to Shorts.
- **Presenter videos** open with your face for a few seconds, then cut to the product. A face in the first
  two seconds earns attention; nobody needs to film it.

## What this does *not* solve

Honesty matters more than a pitch here, so three limits:

- **It will not make a bad product interesting.** The brief is only as good as your site. If your landing
  page cannot say who the product is for, neither can a video.
- **Volume is necessary, not sufficient.** Four posts a week gets you the reps the recommendation model
  needs. Which hooks work for *your* audience is something you learn from the numbers, not something
  anyone can promise up front.
- **The swipe still has to be honest.** If you keep drafts you would never say out loud, the feed
  trains toward posts you do not believe. Skip freely.

## Start smaller than you think

If you are at zero today, do not plan a content strategy. Do this:

1. Paste the URL. Read the brief. Fix the one line that is wrong about your product.
2. Swipe through ten drafts. Keep two. Skip the rest without guilt.
3. Schedule both for this week.
4. Next week, look at one number: which post sent people to your product. Keep two more like it.

Four minutes. The camera stays in the drawer, and the product keeps getting built.
