A short-form video is decided in its first three seconds. Not "mostly decided" — decided. If the opening
line does not earn the next second, nothing after it exists.

When we built Friday's template library we did the boring version of research: we pulled hundreds of
shorts from the solo-builder and indie-app niches that outperformed their creator's median by a wide
margin, and we hand-tagged what each one *did* in its opening. The topics were all over the place. The
openings were not. They collapsed into ten patterns, and those ten now sit under every draft Friday makes.

None of them are clever. All of them are honest about the one thing a viewer wants: a reason to stay.

## The ten, with examples for a software product

**1. Confession → turn.** Admit the slow start, then the one change. *"Six months, zero dollars. Then I
changed one thing about my week."* Works because it is a story with a receipt at the end, and founders
watching are living the first half.

**2. Bold claim + receipts.** A number up front that the body has to back up. *"One post sent 900 people to
my app."* The number is the hook; the receipts are the video.

**3. POV.** Put the viewer inside the after-state. *"POV: your app posts itself while you fix bugs."* Cheap
to write, easy to overdo. One per batch, not five.

**4. Listicle.** Three to five items, and the product is item three, described by what it does, not what
it is called. *"Three tools that fixed my distribution. Nobody talks about the third."*

**5. Unpopular opinion.** Contradict the standard advice, then earn it. *"Your roadmap is the reason nobody
signs up."* Comments arrive; some of them are customers.

**6. Wish I knew.** Lessons framed at the viewer's near future. *"Before you launch a solo SaaS, three
things."* Strongest with an audience that is one step behind you.

**7. Curiosity gap.** Open a question in the first line and pay it off near the end, never in the middle.
*"The fix wasn't in the product."*

**8. Before / after.** The old way and the new way, side by side, numbers if you have them. *"Twelve views
a video. Then four thousand. Same product."*

**9. Day-N build log.** One concrete thing shipped, one number, dated. *"Day 31. Shipped the calendar. 43
signups."* The most sustainable pattern for a builder, because you are already doing the work.

**10. Tool demo.** Show the thing doing the thing, narrated in plain steps. *"Watch it post while I build."*
Skip the intro. The demo is the hook.

## The first three seconds, second by second

Patterns tell you what to say. Timing tells you how. Three rules that every breakout short in our set
obeyed, and that Friday's render follows automatically:

**The line is on screen at 0.0 seconds.** No fade-in, no logo, no "hey guys". The first frame is the hook,
readable, large. A viewer who mutes still gets it.

**The voice says something different from the text.** In the good ones, the on-screen line and the spoken
line are two hooks, not one repeated. Text: *"Six months, zero dollars."* Voice: *"I nearly shut it down
in March."* You get two chances to earn the second.

**By two seconds, there is a promise.** What they get if they stay: a number, a turn, a reveal. The product
does not appear until the promise is made. Products are the payoff, never the hook.

## How to use this without becoming a marketer

You do not need to memorise the list. You need to recognise the patterns when you see them, so you can
keep the right drafts and skip the wrong ones in ten seconds each.

When Friday shows you a pick, it tells you which pattern it is built on. Over a few weeks the tracked-link
clicks tell you which two or three patterns your audience actually responds to. From then on, you keep
those and skip the rest. The library does the writing. You do the judgement.

That is the honest division of labour: patterns from posts that already won, words about your product,
approval from the only person who knows whether the line is true.
